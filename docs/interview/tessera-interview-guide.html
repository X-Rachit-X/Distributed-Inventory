Interview guide · Distributed reservation & inventory infrastructure

Tessera

Allocating scarce inventory to many simultaneous claimants without ever overselling — while payments time out, processes die, and messages arrive twice or out of order.

A tessera was the Roman admission token: one object, one entry, impossible to duplicate. Holding that guarantee under thousands of concurrent requests is the whole system.

01Objective, requirements and non-functional requirements

Start every interview answer here: what it is for, then what it must do, then how well.

The 30-second answer. Tessera is a distributed reservation and inventory platform. The hard problem is allocating scarce inventory to many concurrent users without ever overselling, while surviving payment failures, crashes and partial outages. The core decision is that PostgreSQL is the only authority: a GiST exclusion constraint makes a double-booking impossible to store, so a bug anywhere above the database costs latency, never correctness. I proved it with a lab that runs nine concurrency strategies against the same workload and counts rows in the database afterwards.

Objective

Rebuild an IRCTC-style ticket-booking app (formerly “ScaleRail”) into generic reservation infrastructure. Railway seats are the demo; the same engine models hotel rooms over nights, concert seats, appointment slots and rental units — anything that is a resource occupied over an interval.

03Architecture, and why each decision

Interviewers grade the reasons, not the boxes. Every box here has one.

Browser ─► gateway :4000 rate limit · waiting room · JWT · RBAC · load shedding · proxy ├─► discovery :4006 Kafka-fed read model · Elasticsearch + PG fallback · L1/L2 cache ├─► reservation :4002 booking API + durable saga (hold → pay → confirm) │ ├─► pricing :4007 server-side fares │ ├─► inventory-engine :4001 THE AUTHORITY: constraint, ledger, expiry │ └─► payment :4003 state machine with UNKNOWN; fault-injecting provider ├─► reconciliation :4004 cross-service checks · safe repair · scoreboard └─► notification :4005 Kafka consumer · exactly-once effect · DLQ + replay PostgreSQL 16 (one DB per service) · Kafka KRaft · Redis 7 · Elasticsearch 8 Prometheus · Grafana · OpenTelemetry → Jaeger · Docker Compose · Kubernetes manifests

The life of one booking

  1. Gateway checks the token bucket, the waiting-room admission token, the JWT, and event-loop lag.
  2. Reservation claims the idempotency key, asks pricing for a quote (the client's price is ignored), stores the reservation + saga row + outbox event in one transaction, and returns 202.
  3. The saga worker claims the saga (SKIP LOCKED + lease) and calls inventory-engine /internal/reserve.
  4. Inventory takes sorted row locks on the seats, lazily expires stale holds, inserts HELD allocations — the constraint is the final word — writes ledger rows and an outbox event.
  5. The saga calls payment. Authorised → confirm the hold → issue a booking (TSR-…). Declined → release the hold (compensation). Timeout → PAYMENT_UNKNOWN; the resolver asks the provider.
  6. The outbox relay publishes to Kafka. Notification sends exactly one message; discovery marks the trip dirty and refreshes availability.
  7. Reconciliation later checks every service agrees, and flags anything that doesn't.

Decisions and their reasons

Why is PostgreSQL the source of truth, not Redis?

Allocation is money. PostgreSQL gives durable, transactional, constraint-checked writes; Redis gives speed with weaker durability and failover semantics (a lock can be granted twice across a failover). In the lab the Redis lock did leak once in 1,000, and the constraint rejected it. So Redis is a load filter; the database is the judge.

Why an exclusion constraint instead of application checks?

An application check is only as good as every code path that remembers it — including migration scripts and a hand-run UPDATE at 3 a.m. A constraint applies to all of them. Half-open int4range spans also model segments exactly: alighting at stop 5 and boarding at stop 5 don't overlap.

Why lock rows before inserting, if the constraint already protects you?

Measured: the constraint alone deadlocks under heavy contention (GiST inserts then scans, and waiters form cycles). Sorted SELECT … FOR UPDATE gives contention an orderly queue. The lock is for throughput; the constraint is for correctness. Forget the lock and it gets slow, not wrong.

Why a saga and not two-phase commit?

2PC holds locks across every participant, including a payment provider that doesn't speak XA, for as long as a customer takes to pay. A saga accepts brief inconsistency (a seat is held, not sold) in exchange for no cross-service locks, and every step has a compensation. Reconciliation covers what the saga can miss.

Why is the saga a table and not a call stack?

If the process dies mid-booking, an in-memory saga dies with it and the seat is stranded. A row with a state, a lease and next_run_at survives crashes, can be claimed by any worker, and every transition is a compare-and-swap on the expected state so two workers can't both advance it.

Why an outbox and not publish-after-commit?

COMMIT; publish() → crash between them loses the event publish(); COMMIT → rollback leaves a lie on the bus

The event row commits or vanishes with the business change. It trades a few ms of delay for “never lost” — proven when Kafka was unreachable for hours and 931 events waited and then published.

Why one database per service?

Independent schema evolution and blast radius. No cross-database joins; reconciliation reads others read-only as the one documented exception. Locally they share one PostgreSQL instance for convenience.

Why Elasticsearch and a PostgreSQL fallback?

Search is discovery, not truth; it can be slightly stale and must never block booking. Elasticsearch gives fuzzy matching and speed; a circuit breaker falls back to PostgreSQL word_similarity so search degrades instead of failing. The seat map and the hold always read inventory directly.

Why a separate pricing service?

The client once sent the price. Now the server quotes base × max(0.3, span share) × demand tier, and the quoted price is locked onto the hold — so a tampered request cannot change what is charged.

Why a waiting room in addition to a rate limiter?

A rate limiter protects the server; it hands out access randomly, which rewards whoever retries hardest. A waiting room protects fairness: FIFO position, a signed admission token, and a fixed admission rate the backend can sustain.

05The database schema

PostgreSQL 16, one database per service. The constraints are the business rules.

The central table: allocations (inventory)

allocations(id, resource_id, span int4range, state, hold_id, booking_id, expires_at, price_cents, reason)

EXCLUDE USING gist (resource_id WITH =, span WITH &&)
  WHERE (state IN ('HELD','CONFIRMED','BLOCKED'))   -- never oversell
CHECK held ⇒ expires_at IS NOT NULL                   -- a hold always expires
CHECK confirmed ⇒ booking_id IS NOT NULL               -- every sale is attributable
TRIGGER insert guard: only HELD or BLOCKED                -- no direct CONFIRMED
TRIGGER transition guard: HELD→CONFIRMED|EXPIRED|RELEASED|CANCELLED, CONFIRMED→CANCELLED

Why plain SQL migrations and not an ORM?

Exclusion constraints, range types, partial indexes, triggers and append-only guards are not expressible in Prisma or most ORMs. Migrations are checksummed (editing an applied one is rejected) and run under an advisory lock so two services starting together can't race.

Why per-aggregate sequences instead of one global sequence?

Ordering only matters within an aggregate (one booking, one hold). A per-aggregate row avoids a single hot counter shared by every write.

08Tricky questions an interviewer may ask

Short, honest answers. If you don't know, say what you'd measure.

“If Kafka guarantees exactly-once, why do you need dedupe?”

Kafka's exactly-once is inside Kafka (producer → topic → consumer offset). My side effect is a database write or an email, outside Kafka. So I make the effect idempotent: the processed_events row is written in the same transaction as the effect.

“What happens if the relay publishes and crashes before marking the row published?”

It publishes again: at-least-once. That's why consumers dedupe by event_id. Duplicates are cheap; losses are not.

“What if the hold expires while the payment is being authorised?”

Confirm checks the hold is still ACTIVE in the same transaction. If it expired, confirm fails, the saga refunds/voids the payment, and the reservation fails cleanly. Reconciliation independently flags any “paid but not booked”.

“Your expiry sweeper is down. Are seats stuck?”

No. Reserve lazily expires stale holds on the seats it's locking, inside its own transaction. The sweeper is for freshness, not correctness.

“Why not just use SERIALIZABLE?”

It would be correct but, under 1,000:1 contention, most transactions would abort with serialization failures and retry. A declarative constraint rejects exactly the conflicting row and nothing else; the row lock makes the queue orderly.

“Clock skew — whose clock decides expiry?”

The database's (now()), one clock. Redis TTLs are only cleanup hints.

“How does this scale to 10× traffic?”

Contention is per seat, not global, so different trains don't compete. Services are stateless and horizontally scalable; saga workers use SKIP LOCKED + leases (verified correct with two workers). The next bottleneck is write throughput on one PostgreSQL — partition inventory by event. I haven't measured a scaling curve, and I'd say so.

“A customer is charged but has no booking. Walk me through it.”

Reconciliation's “captured payment without booking” check finds it after a grace window, requires two sightings, and raises a money issue for a human — it never auto-refunds, because an automated refund loop that misfires is worse than the original problem.

“Why is a 409 not an error?”

Under contention, losing is the expected outcome for 999 of 1,000 users. Returning it fast (not after a 30 s timeout) is the whole point of the row-lock queue.

“What's the weakest part of the system?”

One PostgreSQL instance is a single point of failure locally, and the Kubernetes manifests haven't been run on a real cluster. In production I'd use a managed HA PostgreSQL with synchronous replicas.

“How is an idempotency key different from a dedupe id?”

An idempotency key comes from the client and protects an API call (with a request hash so the same key with a different body is rejected). A dedupe id is the event's id and protects a consumer.

10Behavioural questions — ready answers

Each maps to a real challenge above.

Tell me about a time you were wrong.

I wrote predictions down before benchmarking. I thought pessimistic locking would be slow and the constraint alone would be fine. The constraint alone deadlocked 609 times out of 1,000. I changed the production design and kept the wrong prediction published next to the result.

Tell me about your hardest bug.

An “illegal saga transition” that only appeared with two workers. A lease was released when a step started, so another worker could take the saga mid-step. I reproduced it on purpose, kept the lease across the step, made transitions compare-and-swap, and now run e2e with two workers.

Tell me about a time your testing misled you.

My benchmark said a naive strategy never oversold. That contradicted the mechanism, so I distrusted the result, found the harness wasn't creating concurrency, fixed it, and the same strategy then sold one seat 64 times.

How do you handle an ambiguous, large requirement?

The brief was about 90 sections. I turned it into phases with exit gates, ordered correctness first, and marked anything I couldn't build properly as “designed, not built” rather than faking it.

Describe a trade-off.

Saga over 2PC: temporary inconsistency in exchange for no locks held across a human paying — backed by reconciliation. Or one Docker image for eight services: slightly bigger, identical dependencies everywhere.

When did you push back?

Auto-repair of money issues would have been easy. I refused: a misfiring automatic refund is harder to undo than the inconsistency it fixes. Money issues go to a human with a recommendation.

Tell me about a failure under real conditions.

Kafka was unreachable for hours during development. Nothing was lost: 931 events waited in the outbox and published on recovery, matching topic offsets exactly.

How do you prioritise?

Correctness → consistency → failure recovery → observability → performance → scale. The engine and its proof came before the frontend.

What would you do differently?

Validate the concurrency harness against a known-broken strategy before measuring anything; load-test through the gateway earlier; isolate test data in its own database.

How do you make sure your work is correct?

Put the rule in the database, share one set of invariants between tests, benchmark and reconciliation, and assert on stored rows rather than responses.
